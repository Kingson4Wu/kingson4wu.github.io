Discussions about AI often hold two ideas at once: productivity may rise, and workers may still worry about their jobs. Why can both be true?

This isn't something you can wave away with "technology always creates opportunities." If you look more carefully, there are features of this particular wave that make the old reassuring frameworks less applicable than they used to be. And those features are turning an economic structure problem into something that's getting harder to ignore.

## Why This Time Is Different

A familiar account of technological change says that machines replace some work while new demand creates other jobs. Steam power, electricity and the internet all brought such shifts, but adjustment took time and its costs were unevenly shared.

AI has a few characteristics that make that old framework less reliable.

**First: cognitive labor, not just manual labor, is on the table.**

Earlier waves of automation also affected office and professional work. What is distinctive about generative AI is that tasks in programming, design, analysis, customer support and writing are now exposed to automation or augmentation. **Exposure is not the same as displacement**: a [2025 ILO study](https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-2025-update) estimates that roughly one in four jobs worldwide has some exposure, while most are more likely to change than disappear outright.

**Second: AI scales, but using it still has a cost.**

Model capabilities can be replicated at scale, but serving another user still incurs inference, energy, data-processing, review and integration costs. In automatable work, a company may grow without adding staff at the old rate. In work that depends on judgment, trust or physical delivery, demand for people may still grow. Hiring decisions depend on costs, demand and whether AI complements workers; scalability alone does not settle them.

**Third: the "one-person company" narrative is popular but doesn't solve the core problem.**

AI may let one person do more, but the claim that one person can replace a ten-person team depends heavily on the business. Even when production becomes easier, finding customers still requires distribution, trust and attention. Those resources do not become evenly available just because making software or content gets cheaper.

## The Fracture Between Efficiency and Distribution

Put these together and an economic structure problem comes into focus:

> **Productivity is expanding rapidly. The distribution mechanism seems stuck in the previous era.**

One scenario is worth watching: if AI raises output in some sectors while labor income there falls, demand may fail to keep up. That is a hypothesis to test against employment, wage and consumption data, not an established economy-wide fact.

Some roles may shrink; others may produce more with AI, while new demand could create different work. The demand shortfall I worry about would arise if job and income losses spread widely and were not offset elsewhere. The Great Depression analogy can help frame the question, but it cannot establish that history will repeat itself.

AI can amplify both capital and labor. My concern is the scenario in which the gains accrue mainly to a small group of asset owners.

Training and deploying foundation models requires compute, data and capital, which favors scale. Open models, applications and productivity gains inside organizations can also benefit workers and smaller firms. Who captures the gains depends on competition, bargaining power and policy; the technical architecture does not dictate a single outcome. The [IMF's labor-market analysis](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2024/01/14/Gen-AI-Artificial-Intelligence-and-the-Future-of-Work-542379) likewise distinguishes substitution from complementarity.

## Structural Consequences

From a macro perspective, a few structural shifts are worth watching.

**Middle-skill pathways may come under pressure.**

If firms use AI to take on some junior and mid-level tasks, the positions where people first build experience may narrow. Effects will differ across sectors and places. A thinner middle is a risk to watch, not yet an established economy-wide result.

**Mobility paths could narrow; individual choices are only part of the story.**

Explaining every missed opportunity as a failure of individual effort ignores the structure of available jobs. Explaining every change through AI alone misses differences between sectors and places. Senior roles are limited, while junior roles are how many people gain experience. If that entry path narrows, mobility could suffer. The scale of that risk still needs employment evidence.

**How public contributions relate to private gains.**

Open-source code, technical blogs and community discussions make a great deal of knowledge publicly available. Some of that material may also enter model training data. Which material was used, under what license, and whether contributors share in the value depends on the dataset and product; there is no single answer. Still, when a commercial product benefits from public contributions while contributors have little say in the returns, the distribution question is real.

## How the System Might Adjust

A practical question: if this structural imbalance persists, can the economy function normally?

If displacement spreads and productivity gains do not flow back through wages, lower prices, investment or public services, weak purchasing power could become a problem. This scenario depends on several conditions. It is a reason to examine distribution, not a firm prediction about the economy.

Historically, similar imbalances seem to have been resolved through some form of social adjustment. The industrial era produced labor laws, unions, social safety nets. The post-Depression era produced government intervention and welfare systems. These adjustments didn't emerge voluntarily — they were forced out by crisis.

Possible repair paths for the AI era, from a purely logical standpoint:

- More aggressive income redistribution, including taxation on technology gains
- UBI — basic income for those outside the employment system
- Deliberately created inefficient roles — employment as a goal, not efficiency
- Waiting for a new demand layer to emerge and drive a fresh employment structure

The last path is especially uncertain. Policy responses do not follow a fixed timetable: some emerge after crises, while others grow from earlier experiments and negotiation. Because the outcome is not predetermined, it makes sense to discuss distribution and support for workers now.

## Closing Reflection

None of this is meant to express pessimism or rationalize the status quo.

Technology is advancing — that's objective. AI genuinely does things that weren't possible before. But improvements in productive capacity and "improvements in most people's living conditions" aren't automatically connected. There's a layer of distribution mechanism in between, and that layer is currently lagging behind the pace of technological development.

This isn't a technology bubble — the capabilities are real. But it's more like this: the distribution logic of technology's gains is being overestimated, while the difficulty of finding a way out for displaced groups is being underestimated.

Understanding the structure, at least, lets you drink a little less of the Kool-Aid. Phrases like "just embrace change and you'll win" are empty. Being realistic is more useful than performing optimism.

## A Question Worth Asking

Structural pain has appeared repeatedly in history. Someone always pays a price for a given era's shift. This isn't the first time, and it won't be the last.

But "it will happen" and "it must happen in the most painful way possible" are two different things.

History has seen gentler adjustments and more violent reorganizations. The difference often has less to do with the technology itself and more to do with when and how society intervenes in the distribution mechanism. Technological progress is objective, but its social consequences aren't fixed — they're shaped by institutional choices, and institutions are made by people, not natural laws.

So the question worth asking may not be "will AI bring pain?" but rather: **can this process be made gentler?** Can we get to a place where, as technology runs ahead, the distribution mechanism is also forced to keep pace — rather than waiting until the cracks are too large to patch before we start?

"That's how it's always been" has never been a valid argument for "that's how it must be."

If civilization continues to develop, I hope it can treat this as a question worth asking — not as a question that's already been answered.
